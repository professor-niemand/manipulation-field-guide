Start here

How the guide works, how to tell influence from manipulation, and how to read the protective responses.

Introduction

The taxonomy is organised into five categories, each covering distinct psychological levers. Each technique is defined, illustrated with a concrete example, and paired with a protective response. Later sections scale the same five categories up to propaganda and algorithmic manipulation, before closing with a personal counter-manipulation framework you can put into practice directly.

Each technique follows the same layout: a definition, an example, a protective response, a caution where there is a specific risk and, where there is a good story behind the term, a Did you know? note. The two internalised patterns in section 2.5 use “What helps” and reflection prompts instead of a protective response, and Gaslighting and Time Pressure also list variants. Services for patterns that can be part of abuse, fraud or coercion are listed together in “Where to get help” at the end of this guide. “How to use the responses” explains how to read the responses.

You don’t need to read this from start to finish. Look up a technique when you meet it, or go straight to the Personal Counter-Manipulation Framework near the end.

It is a teaching guide, not therapy, legal or professional advice, and if you are in danger it is no substitute for specialist help. How it was made, and what was and wasn’t checked, is in “How this guide was made” at the end.

Persuasion, Influence, or Manipulation?

Persuasion is the act of trying to move someone towards a decision or belief. How it’s done determines what to call it: done with transparency and respect for the other person’s autonomy, it’s influence; done by hiding information, applying emotional pressure, or working around someone’s judgement rather than through it, it’s manipulation. Before working through the techniques in this guide, it helps to have a way of telling the two apart — because they often use the exact same psychological levers.

To tell the difference, ask the following about a specific exchange. If the answer is yes, it leans towards manipulation; if no, towards influence:

  • Is information being hidden or distorted?
  • Is emotional pressure used instead of logic?
  • Do you lose autonomy or clarity afterwards?
  • Does the persuader benefit more than you, long-term?
  • Is their real reason for wanting this hidden from you — even if every fact they’ve told you checks out?

In essence: influence seeks informed consent; manipulation seeks unconscious compliance.

One important caveat: manipulation isn’t always deliberate. Many of these patterns are learned early — as children, we pick up guilt-tripping, silent treatment, or minimising as ways to get needs met or to protect ourselves, long before we have the words for what we’re doing. By adulthood, a lot of that has become habit: a default response under stress, or a way to avoid conflict, rather than a calculated strategy. Someone using a technique in this guide isn’t necessarily a manipulator in the deliberate, scheming sense — they may simply be running on a pattern nobody ever taught them to notice. The test above still applies, because the effect on you is the same either way. You can’t see inside anyone’s head, so don’t try to decide whether it’s deliberate. If someone has been calm and safe when you’ve raised things before, you might name a pattern gently and give them room to recognise it. If you’re unsure, or you feel afraid, keep your distance and stick to the private steps in the protective responses.

How to use the responses

Every technique has a definition, an example and a protective response. Some also have a caution or a note on where the term comes from.

  • Protective response: steps chosen to stay low-risk if the other person reacts badly. Most are things you do privately: note, check, pause, decide later. Where a line is quoted, it is short and non-confrontational. None asks you to confront anyone or explain the tactic they are using.
  • Caution: appears only where there is a specific risk.
  • Where to get help: services for patterns that can be part of abuse, fraud or coercion, listed together at the end of the guide. Many techniques can occur in abusive relationships, so use it even where an entry has no caution. The first line of each topic applies anywhere. The line marked UK gives UK numbers, findahelpline.com lists services in other countries.

No response is risk-free with someone who is volatile or controlling. If you are afraid of the person, put your safety first and use the services at the end of this guide.

If you are safe and want to speak to someone directly, keep it short: describe the specific behaviour, say what you need, once, and stop if they escalate. Do this only with someone who has stayed calm when you’ve raised concerns before, doesn’t punish or retaliate afterwards, and isn’t someone you depend on for housing, money, a visa or your safety. If you’re unsure, treat them as not safe and stay with the private steps.

Two rules apply throughout. Never explain someone’s tactic to a person who is volatile, controlling or holds power over you, because naming it can make things worse. And you can’t see anyone’s intent: use this guide to understand what is happening to you, not to diagnose other people or as a weapon in an argument.
