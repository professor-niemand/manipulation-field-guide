## How this guide was made {#how-this-guide-was-made}

**What I did.** I came up with the idea, the five-category structure, the choice of techniques and the approach to the protective responses, and I made the final decision on every entry. I didn’t invent the techniques themselves: they come from psychology, sociology and rhetoric, and from the books and papers listed here. My aim was concise, structured tools that help people recognise manipulation and respond without harming others or themselves.

**What AI did.** Over more than a year I used several AI models (ChatGPT, Claude, DeepSeek, Gemini, Llama, Mistral and Qwen) to draft entries, examples and trivia, suggest edits, spot gaps and check wording. I reconsidered and revised every entry several times. I didn’t record which wording came from which tool, so I can’t say that passage by passage.

**Why it is free.** This guide stands on other people’s research and was written with the help of AI, so it did not feel right to charge for it. In my opinion, the people who most need a guide like this are often the ones who can least afford one.

**Sources.** In October and November 2026 Claude checked the source lists. Of 86 entries, 43 (listed under Sources) were checked against the paper, abstract, reference page or official record itself. The other 43 (listed under Other sources) had their details confirmed, but the text was not read line by line; many are books, including those recommended under Further Reading. Some wording in the guide was corrected as a result. I have not independently verified these checks, and AI checking can still miss mistakes. If you find one, send me a source and I’ll correct it.

**Not covered.** The examples, protective responses, cautions and support details added or rewritten in October and November 2026 were not part of that source check, and no domestic abuse, legal or clinical specialist has reviewed them. Claude checked the phone numbers and legal points against published pages on 31 October 2026; several came from council or NHS directory pages rather than the organisations’ own sites. Please check them before relying on them.

**Limits.** This is a teaching guide, not a scientific paper, and not therapy, legal or professional advice. Some research cited here is disputed, and the text says where.

**Safety.** If you are in an abusive or controlling relationship, using these protective responses with that person could make things worse. Naming what they are doing is risky, and leaving can be the most dangerous time, so speak to a specialist service before you act. In England, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free and open 24 hours on 0808 2000 247, and Men’s Advice Line is on 0808 801 0327. More numbers are in “Where to get help” at the end of the guide. In immediate danger call your local emergency number (999 in the UK). From a UK mobile, if you can’t speak, stay on the line and press 55 when prompted. Outside the UK, findahelpline.com lists services by country. If someone may check your phone, browser or accounts, use a device they can’t access, such as a library computer or a friend’s phone, and don’t rely on clearing your history to hide a visit.
