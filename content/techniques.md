Five categories, each built from techniques that share a common lever — starting with how manipulation distorts your grip on reality itself.

# 1 Psychological Distortion

These tactics distort your perception of reality and undermine your sense of what’s real or true. If someone controls your version of reality, they don’t need to control your actions directly.

## 1.1 Reality Manipulation

Tactics that alter facts or your interpretation of them.

### 1.1.1 Gaslighting {#gaslighting}

**Definition:** Repeatedly denying or distorting shared reality until the target doubts their own memory, perception, or sanity — not just a single fact, but their ability to trust their own mind.

**Variants:** Denial: repeatedly denying something that did happen. Implantation: subtly introducing false recollections until the target accepts them as genuine memories.

**Example (Denial):** They deny something you clearly remember — ‘I never said that, you’re imagining things’ — every time you raise it, until you start doubting your own memory before you’ve even checked it.

**Example (Implantation):** “Don’t you remember? You agreed to this at dinner last month.” — said with enough confidence and detail that the target begins to doubt their own recall.

**Protective Response:** Keep a dated private record of what was said and when, somewhere they can’t reach, such as a notebook kept elsewhere or a friend’s inbox, not a device or account they can open. Don’t argue the facts. Check your memory with someone independent.

**Did you know?** The term “gaslighting” comes from the 1938 stage play Gas Light by Patrick Hamilton. A husband secretly searches the flat upstairs, which dims the gas lights below, then denies the change, making his wife question her own perception of reality. A real 1995 study by psychologist Elizabeth Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell showed how easily a false memory can be planted: they suggested a false childhood memory (“you got lost in a shopping centre”) to 24 participants — about a quarter of them reported remembering it.

### 1.1.2 Minimising {#minimising}

**Definition:** Downplaying your emotions, experiences, or harm done to make the issue seem trivial.

**Example:** “You’re overreacting, it was just a joke.”

**Protective Response:** State the impact once and don’t debate its size.

## 1.2 Moral Inversion

Twists the moral lens — turns wrong into right and vice versa — to shift blame.

### 1.2.1 Projection {#projection}

**Definition:** Accusing others of the very behaviour or motive the manipulator is guilty of.

**Example:** A liar calling you dishonest because you caught them.

**Protective Response:** Don’t defend yourself or counter-accuse. Check the facts privately. Say “I don’t see it that way,” then move on.

**Did you know?** Projection was Sigmund Freud’s own concept, introduced in his 1890s writings on paranoia and one of the earliest defence mechanisms in psychoanalysis. His daughter, Anna Freud, later listed it alongside repression, regression, reaction formation and sublimation in her 1936 systematic account of the ego’s defences.

### 1.2.2 Playing Victim {#playing-victim}

**Definition:** Pretending to be the injured party to dodge accountability or gain sympathy.

**Example:** Asked why they missed your event, they tear up: “I always let everyone down.”

**Protective Response:** Acknowledge the feeling once, don’t comfort them out of the issue, and repeat the one thing you need.

### 1.2.3 DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender) {#darvo}

**Definition:** A compound tactic in which the perpetrator denies the behaviour, attacks the person doing the confronting, and then claims to be the true victim (a form of Playing Victim, here combined with denial and attack) — all in rapid sequence. The goal is to make the person raising the issue feel like the aggressor.

**Example:** Confronted about yelling during a meeting, they respond: “I never yelled. You’re the one who always provokes me, and now you’re trying to make me look abusive. I’m the one being treated unfairly here.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t answer the three moves. Say one sentence, end the conversation, talk it through afterwards with someone outside before deciding whether you were in the wrong.

**Caution:** Naming the pattern to a volatile person is likely to escalate it. Don’t quote the sequence back to them. A DARVO-style reaction is also not proof of guilt, so use the pattern to understand what is happening to you, not as a verdict on them. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control, Work.

**Did you know?** DARVO was named by psychologist Jennifer Freyd in a 1997 paper, describing a pattern she had observed specifically in how perpetrators respond when confronted. A 2017 study of people who had confronted someone found that DARVO responses were common, that the three parts tended to appear together, and that more exposure to DARVO went with more self-blame in the person who had confronted.

### 1.2.4 Whataboutism {#whataboutism}

**Definition:** Deflecting a valid criticism by pointing to an unrelated wrongdoing committed by the accuser or a third party, rather than addressing the original claim. Distinct from Projection, which falsely attributes the manipulator’s own behaviour to you, and from DARVO, which is a compound deny-attack-reverse sequence — whataboutism is a single deflective move that doesn’t deny the original act, it just changes the subject.

**Example:** “You’re criticising my spending? What about the money you wasted last year?”

**Protective Response:** “Fair, we can look at that separately,” then return to the point once. If it loops, stop.

**Did you know?** The underlying move is classical: philosophers named it tu quoque (“you, too”) centuries before “whataboutism” existed as a word — it’s one of the oldest deflection tactics in rhetoric.

### 1.2.5 Blame-Shifting {#blame-shifting}

**Definition:** Dodging responsibility by pinning the cause of their behaviour on you. Distinct from Playing Victim, which claims injury rather than assigning cause.

**Example:** “I only yelled because you made me angry.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t debate what caused it. If it’s heated, step out and come back later. Afterwards, separate what you did from what they chose to do, ideally by talking it through with someone you trust.

**Caution:** If you feel afraid when this happens, treat it as Intimidation, not a discussion to win. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control, Danger or threats.

### 1.2.6 Weaponised Incompetence {#weaponised-incompetence}

**Definition:** Deliberately doing a task poorly or slowly until it’s taken away and given to someone else — offloading the responsibility while still looking like you tried. Distinct from Stonewalling, which refuses outright, and Neglect Punishment, which withdraws attention as retaliation: this one performs badly rather than refusing or withdrawing.

***Example:*** “I tried to do the laundry but I shrank all your sweaters” — repeated until you stop asking them to do the laundry.

**Protective Response:** Decide beforehand whether you’ll accept an imperfect result or hand over a clear spec with a date, such as “Can you do the laundry this week, colours and whites separate?” Don’t take the task back in frustration. Whether it’s deliberate or genuine struggle, the fix is the same. With a controlling partner, or if you are afraid of their reaction, don’t press the point; see “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control.

## 1.3 Cognitive Distortion

Abuses logic or reason to distort perception of risk or morality.

### 1.3.1 Fear-Mongering {#fear-mongering}

**Definition:** Exaggerating threats or dangers to steer decisions through fear. Typically no odds or evidence are given.

**Example:** “Skip the extended warranty and one breakdown could ruin you.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t decide while scared. Pause and check the real odds with a source they don’t control.

**Did you know?** Fear appeals are not manipulative in themselves. A 2000 meta-analysis found that strong fear messages changed behaviour most when they came with a clear, achievable action, and provoked the most defensive reactions, such as avoidance, when they did not. Later reviews disagree about how well fear appeals work overall.

### 1.3.2 False Equivalence {#false-equivalence}

**Definition:** Claiming two unequal things are the same to flatten moral or factual differences.

**Example:** “Lying on your résumé is just like forgetting to return a call.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t argue over whether they’re the same. Privately compare intent, harm and frequency.

### 1.3.3 Mislabelling {#mislabelling}

**Definition:** Using emotionally loaded or inaccurate terms to reframe perception.

**Example:** You ask for a week’s notice before schedule changes, and they say “You’re so controlling.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t accept the label or debate the word. Describe what you asked for, once, in plain terms.

**Did you know?** In a 1981 experiment, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described the same disease-control programme to two groups of people, as either “200 people will be saved” or “400 people will die”. Of those given the first wording, 72% chose it; of those given the second, only 22% chose the identical outcome.

### 1.3.4 Slippery Slope Fallacy {#slippery-slope-fallacy}

**Definition:** Claiming that one small step will inevitably lead to an extreme and undesirable outcome, without providing evidence for the causal chain in between. Distinct from Fear-Mongering, which inflates one danger, whereas this strings steps together.

**Example:** “Let one person work from home on Fridays and soon nobody will come in at all.”

**Protective Response:** “I’ll think about it,” then decide on your own evidence. Don’t answer the chain. If nobody can show the link, it’s an assertion, not evidence.

### 1.3.5 Moving the Goalposts {#moving-the-goalposts}

**Definition:** Retroactively changing the criteria for success after the original criteria have already been met, so the target can never actually win. Distinct from Slippery Slope Fallacy, which projects a hypothetical future chain of consequences — this instead redefines a standard that’s already been satisfied, in the present.

**Example:** “I’ll trust you again once you’re on time for a month.” A month passes. ”Well, being on time isn’t the same as actually caring — show me you care.”

**Protective Response:** Where it is safe (see the caution below), get the agreed bar stated in writing, with a date. If the bar keeps moving whatever you do, the bar isn’t the real issue, so stop trying to meet it and talk to someone outside.

**Caution:** Getting the bar in writing suits work, landlords and contracts. With a controlling partner it can escalate, and it won’t stop the bar moving. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control.

### 1.3.6 Straw Man Argument {#straw-man-argument}

**Definition:** Misrepresenting or oversimplifying someone’s argument into a weaker, more extreme version, then attacking that version instead of what was actually said. Distinct from False Equivalence, which equates two unlike things, and Mislabelling, which relabels something with a loaded term — strawmanning specifically distorts the content of an argument before attacking it.

***Example:*** You say “I think we should double-check the numbers before deciding.” They respond: “So you don’t trust anyone on this team and want to slow everything down?”

**Protective Response:** Restate your point once calmly, or let it go. If they keep distorting it, stop correcting.

# 2 Emotional Manipulation

These tactics exploit emotional vulnerabilities to create dependence or compliance. If someone can make you feel what they want, they don’t need to argue for it.

## 2.1 Affection Exploitation

Tactics that weaponise warmth, care, or attraction.

### 2.1.1 Love-Bombing {#love-bombing}

**Definition:** Overwhelming you with affection, attention, or praise to create dependency or fast trust. The intensity runs well ahead of how long they’ve known you.

**Example:** “You’re the most amazing person I’ve ever met — I’ve never felt this way about anyone,” said on day 3.

**Protective Response:** Slow the pace, keep your own friends and routines, and tell someone you trust about the relationship early.

**Caution:** If they sulk, push or escalate, treat that as a warning sign and keep your outside contacts. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control, Scams and fraud.

**Did you know?** The term is usually traced to members of the Unification Church (“the Moonies”) in the 1970s, who used it approvingly for how they welcomed newcomers, before psychologists borrowed it to describe abusive relationships.

### 2.1.2 Conditional Regard {#conditional-regard}

**Definition:** Giving approval or affection only when you comply or behave as desired. Distinct from Variable Approval, where the swings don’t track anything you did.

**Example:** They’re warm and supportive when you agree, cold or dismissive when you don’t.

**Protective Response:** Notice the pattern privately over a few weeks, keep friendships that don’t depend on them, and check your read with someone outside.

**Did you know?** This is the opposite of psychologist Carl Rogers’ “unconditional positive regard” — a cornerstone of his person-centred therapy from the 1950s. Research on parents who give or withhold approval depending on behaviour links it to resentment and swings in self-esteem in their children.

### 2.1.3 Hoovering {#hoovering}

**Definition:** Re-engaging a discarded or distanced target with sudden affection, nostalgia, or false hope of change, in order to pull them back into contact or the relationship. Distinct from Love-Bombing, which front-loads a new relationship with intensity to build trust fast — hoovering specifically happens after a breakup, estrangement, or period of no contact, to reopen it.

***Example:*** Weeks of silence after a breakup, then out of nowhere: “I’ve been in therapy. I’ve changed. Can we talk?”

**Protective Response:** Don’t reply in the moment. Judge by what they’ve done over time, not how the message feels. If no-contact was your boundary, keep it.

**Caution:** If you left because of abuse or you fear them, don’t handle this alone. Keep the messages and ask a domestic abuse service before replying. The period after leaving can be the riskiest. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control, Danger or threats.

**Did you know?** The term is usually said to come from the Hoover vacuum cleaner brand — the metaphor being that the manipulator tries to “suck” the target back into their orbit once a source of attention or control has been lost.

### 2.1.4 Future Faking {#future-faking}

**Definition:** Making detailed, emotionally compelling promises about a shared future — marriage, moving in together, changed behaviour — with no genuine intention of following through, in order to secure compliance or commitment in the present. Distinct from Bait-and-Switch, where the switch happens immediately after commitment — future faking keeps the promise perpetually deferred, so the “switch” never has to formally happen at all. Look for big promises about later with nothing concrete now.

***Example:*** Detailed plans for a future together are described early and often — but every time a concrete step would need to happen, something “isn’t the right time yet.”

**Protective Response:** Weigh what they do now above what they promise. Ask for one small, near-term step and see if it’s kept before investing more.

**Did you know?** The underlying mechanism — hope that the good version of someone will return, keeping you invested in an unequal relationship — echoes what researchers Donald Dutton and Susan Painter called traumatic bonding in their 1981 paper on relationships involving intermittent abuse: attachment that grows from a power imbalance combined with alternating good and bad treatment.

## 2.2 Guilt & Shame Induction

Using your conscience against you.

### 2.2.1 Guilt-Tripping {#guilt-tripping}

**Definition:** Manipulating your sense of duty or gratitude to make you do something.

**Example:** “After all I’ve done for you, you can’t even do this one thing?”

**Protective Response:** “I’ll think about it.” Decide later, away from them, on whether you want to, not whether you owe it.

### 2.2.2 Shame Induction {#shame-induction}

**Definition:** Attacking identity rather than behaviour — making the target feel fundamentally defective as a person rather than situationally wrong — to inhibit pushback. Unlike guilt-tripping, which targets what you did, shame induction targets who you are.

**Example:** “A decent person wouldn’t even question this. What does that say about you?”

**Protective Response:** Don’t defend your character. Step away and talk to someone who knows you, since shame pushes people to isolate.

**Caution:** If this is routine from someone you live with, a support service is worth contacting. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control, Wellbeing.

**Did you know?** Psychologist Helen Block Lewis drew this distinction in 1971, and later researchers summed it up this way: guilt says “I did something wrong”, shame says “I am wrong”. Guilt targets the act; shame targets the person.

## 2.3 Emotional Withdrawal

Control through silence or absence.

### 2.3.1 Silent Treatment {#silent-treatment}

**Definition:** Withholding communication or affection to punish or regain dominance.

**Example:** Days of ignored messages after a disagreement.

**Protective Response:** Don’t chase, flood messages or apologise just to end it. Keep your routine. One calm message such as “I’m here when you want to talk” is enough; then stop.

**Did you know?** Silence was used as a formal punishment in 19th-century prisons: at Auburn Prison in New York, inmates were forbidden to speak to one another from the 1820s. Research led by Naomi Eisenberger, with Matthew Lieberman and Kipling Williams (2003) shows that being ignored activates some of the same brain regions as physical pain, though later work suggests the brain doesn’t process the two in exactly the same way.

### 2.3.2 Neglect Punishment {#neglect-punishment}

**Definition:** Subtle withdrawal — attention, intimacy, inclusion — until you “earn” them back. It typically starts after you assert yourself and is never announced.

**Example:** They suddenly stop inviting you or showing warmth after you assert yourself.

**Protective Response:** Keep your normal behaviour and other relationships. Mark when it happens against what you said or did just before, to see whether there is a pattern.

## 2.4 Charm & Reward Tactics

Pleasure instead of pressure.

### 2.4.1 Flattery {#flattery}

**Definition:** Excessive compliments or charisma used to lower defences. Praise tends to arrive right before an ask.

**Example:** “You’re so smart — I trust you to understand what I mean,” right before the ask.

**Protective Response:** Say thanks and decide on the ask separately, later, as if no compliment had been made.

**Did you know?** Aristotle drew a similar line over 2,300 years ago in the Nicomachean Ethics: someone pleasant with no ulterior aim is merely obsequious, but the flatterer is pleasant for the sake of some advantage and, in Aristotle’s words, “a friend in an inferior position, or pretends to be such and to love more than he is loved”.

### 2.4.2 Reciprocity Priming {#reciprocity-priming}

**Definition:** Offering an unsolicited favour, gift, or concession first, so you feel an unspoken obligation to repay it — often with something far larger than what was given.

**Example:** “Here, I got this for you, no reason” — followed days later by a favour asked “between friends,” sized well beyond the original gift.

**Protective Response:** Judge the gift and the ask separately. A gift carries no invoice, and accepting it doesn’t create a debt.

**Did you know?** This is a small-scale version of what psychologist Robert Cialdini called the principle of reciprocity, one of the most powerful drivers of compliance his research identified.

### 2.4.3 Negging {#negging}

**Definition:** A backhanded compliment or casual put-down, calibrated to sting just enough to make the target seek the manipulator’s approval — while staying deniable enough to pass as an offhand remark. Distinct from Flattery, which lowers defences with praise — negging works the opposite way, using a small insult to create the itch for approval rather than granting it.

**Example:** “You look great — for someone who doesn’t work out.” Enough sting to prompt a reaction, enough plausible deniability to call it a compliment if challenged.

**Protective Response:** Notice the urge to prove yourself, because that urge is the mechanism. Don’t chase their approval, and pull back if it repeats.

**Did you know?** Popularised by the pickup-artist community and documented in Neil Strauss’s 2005 book The Game, where it’s described as a way of lowering someone’s guard by denying them easy validation.

## 2.5 Internalised Manipulation

When someone has been subjected to manipulation long enough, they begin applying the manipulator’s patterns to themselves — pre-emptively apologising, doubting their own perceptions, or shrinking their behaviour before being asked to. This is not a tactic used by the manipulator; it is its residue in the target, and it can persist long after the relationship ends. Read these two as questions to ask yourself, not as patterns to spot in others.

### 2.5.1 Self-Gaslighting {#self-gaslighting}

**Definition:** Doubting your own perceptions, memories, or emotional responses as a result of prolonged external gaslighting. The manipulator no longer needs to deny your reality — you do it for them.

**Example:** “I’m probably overreacting again. I always misread situations.” — said internally, despite having legitimate and well-founded concerns.

**What helps:** Write down what you observed before interpreting it, then check it with a trusted person who wasn’t there.

**Reflection prompts:** “Have I written off a reaction as overreacting before checking what happened?” “Does the doubt show up before I’ve even spoken to them?”

### 2.5.2 Pre-emptive Appeasement {#pre-emptive-appeasement}

**Definition:** Modifying your behaviour or speech in anticipation of a reaction that has not yet occurred — effectively doing the manipulator’s work for them. Also called the fawn response in trauma literature.

**Example:** Softening feedback you give to a third party because you unconsciously fear the manipulator’s disapproval — even when the manipulator isn’t present or involved.

**What helps:** Pause before self-censoring. If it’s everywhere in your life, a doctor or a therapist who knows relational trauma can help.

**Reflection prompts:** “Am I doing this because it’s right, or because I’m afraid of a reaction?” “Would I act this way if they weren’t watching?”

**Caution:** If this is a response to a real threat, the problem is the threat, not you. See “Where to get help”: Wellbeing, Abuse or coercive control.

**Did you know?** The “fawn response” was described by therapist Pete Walker in a 2003 article, then developed in his 2013 book Complex PTSD: From Surviving to Thriving.

# 3 Communication Control

These tactics restrict, distort or reroute communication to dominate the narrative. Whoever controls the channel controls the outcome.

## 3.1 Information Blockage

Deliberate suppression of communication or access to facts.

### 3.1.1 Stonewalling {#stonewalling}

**Definition:** Refusal to engage in normal dialogue — cutting off discussions, answers, or feedback loops. Distinct from Silent Treatment, which is a total communication blackout used punitively — stonewalling is narrower: refusing to engage on one specific issue, often while other communication continues normally.

**Example:** You bring up a concern and they simply stop responding or say “I don’t want to talk about this” every time.

**Protective Response:** Say once that it matters to you, suggest a time, then step back. Don’t beg for basic communication.

**Caution:** If the off-limits topics are money, where you can go or who you see, and you get no say, that’s control, not avoidance. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control.

**Did you know?** Psychologist John Gottman identified stonewalling as one of his “Four Horsemen” of relationship breakdown, alongside criticism, contempt and defensiveness — drawn from his decades of marital-stability research.

### 3.1.2 Withholding Information {#withholding-information}

**Definition:** Selectively keeping key facts secret to control decisions or outcomes.

**Example:** Your manager knows the client has dropped the project and says nothing while you spend a week on it.

**Protective Response:** Ask a closed question in writing, so the answer is on record. Check a second source.

**Caution:** A partner who won’t let you see household money or documents is a recognised form of control. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control, Money control, Work.

## 3.2 Narrative Shaping

Redirecting how messages travel or how stories get told.

### 3.2.1 Triangulation {#triangulation}

**Definition:** Using a third party to communicate indirectly or influence perception.

**Example:** “Alex said you were upset with me” — when Alex wasn’t part of the conflict.

**Protective Response:** Treat it as unverified. Don’t interrogate Alex or pass it on.

**Did you know?** The idea comes from psychiatrist Murray Bowen’s family systems theory, in which he described emotional “triangles”. He began developing it in the 1950s and continued at Georgetown University from 1959.

### 3.2.2 Spinning Partial Truths {#spinning-partial-truths}

**Definition:** Presenting selectively chosen facts or a biased framing to mislead without technically lying. Distinct from Withholding Information, which keeps facts fully secret — this instead discloses some facts while framing them to mislead, without an outright lie.

**Example:** “We hit our goals this quarter,” (leaving out that they changed the goals halfway through).

**Protective Response:** Find the original figures or earlier statement yourself. Don’t call it lying, because each fact checks out.

**Did you know?** “Spin doctor” has its earliest known printed use in a New York Times editorial of 21 October 1984 — but the practice of shading a story rather than lying outright is as old as politics itself.

### 3.2.3 Rewriting History {#rewriting-history}

**Definition:** Altering the record of what was agreed, decided, or done — after the fact — so that other people (a team, an organisation, a future reader) come to believe a different version of events. Unlike gaslighting, the goal isn’t to make the target doubt their own mind — the target can be completely certain of what happened and still lose, because the manipulation targets the evidence, not the person.

**Example:** “We never agreed to that timeline” — even though the email thread everyone else saw clearly shows they did. The minutes get “corrected” after the fact, and only the new version gets circulated.

**Protective Response:** Confirm agreements in writing straight after, to everyone involved: “Just confirming what we agreed: X.” Check your workplace’s rules before copying work emails to a personal account.

**Caution:** If it affects pay or discipline, ask a union representative or an employment adviser before replying. See “Where to get help”: Work.

**Did you know?** The tactic is ancient. Hatshepsut’s name and image were chiselled off her monuments, apparently on the orders of her successor, Thutmose III, but not until about twenty years after she died, and historians still debate why; a 2025 study of her broken statues argues they were ritually “deactivated” rather than destroyed in hatred. The Latin term damnatio memoriae, “condemnation of memory”, is often applied to this kind of erasure. The Soviet Union did the same with photographs: former NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov, arrested in 1939 and executed in 1940, was airbrushed out of an official photo of him walking beside Stalin along the Moscow–Volga Canal. That pair of images gave its name to David King’s book on Soviet photo-faking, The Commissar Vanishes.

## 3.3 Isolation Mechanisms

Preventing collaboration or mutual validation of truth.

### 3.3.1 Divide and Conquer {#divide-and-conquer}

**Definition:** Turning people against each other or isolating you from allies to maintain control. Distinct from Triangulation, which uses a third party as an indirect channel on one specific issue — this works more broadly, turning people against each other or cutting you off from allies. A typical sign is hearing different stories from different people, so you can’t compare.

**Example:** A manipulative boss tells each employee different versions of the same story so they can’t unite.

**Protective Response:** Keep your own record of who told you what, and when. Compare notes only privately with people you trust, and don’t repeat what you’ve heard as fact.

**Caution:** Comparing notes about a manager can backfire at work, so consider a formal route (HR, union). In a relationship, being cut off from friends or family is a control warning sign. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control, Work.

**Did you know?** Divide et impera is traditionally attributed to Philip II of Macedon (Alexander the Great’s father) in the 4th century BC. Machiavelli’s The Art of War (1521) later advised commanders to divide the enemy’s forces, though not in those words.

### 3.3.2 Smear Campaigns {#smear-campaigns}

**Definition:** Spreading rumours or half-truths to damage your credibility before you can speak for yourself.

**Example:** They quietly tell others you’re “unstable” right after you call out their behaviour.

**Protective Response:** Don’t chase every rumour. Keep an evidence file of screenshots, dates and witnesses, and tell the few people who matter, once, factually.

**Caution:** Serious false claims may count as defamation or harassment. Keep evidence and get legal advice early. See “Where to get help”: Harassment or defamation, Work.

# 4 Coercive Pressure

These tactics force or rush compliance through external stressors. When logic fails, fear and time pressure fill the gap.

## 4.1 Threat-Based Coercion

Forcing compliance through fear or intimidation.

### 4.1.1 Intimidation / Threats {#intimidation-threats}

**Definition:** Creating real or implied danger (social, financial, physical) to make you yield.

**Example:** “If you go against me, you’ll regret it.” A quieter version: “It would be a shame if people found out how you really are.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t argue, bargain or confront. In immediate danger, call your local emergency number. Save the exact words, the time and any messages or witnesses, somewhere they can’t reach.

**Caution:** If the threat comes from a partner or someone you live with, leaving can be the riskiest moment. Contact a domestic abuse helpline for safety planning before you act. At work, use the formal route (HR, a written complaint), not a confrontation. See “Where to get help”: Danger or threats, Abuse or coercive control, Work.

### 4.1.2 Blackmail {#blackmail}

**Definition:** Using threatened exposure (private information, images, secrets) to force you to act or pay. Distinct from general Intimidation/Threats, which can threaten any kind of harm — blackmail specifically uses threatened exposure of private information as its lever.

**Example:** “Pay me or I’ll send these photos to your contacts.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t pay, since payment usually brings more demands. Don’t delete anything, and stop replying. Report to the police and the platform, and change passwords and 2FA.

**Caution:** If the person is under 18, tell a trusted adult. If the blackmailer is a partner or ex, treat it as abuse too. See “Where to get help”: Intimate images, Danger or threats, Abuse or coercive control.

**Did you know?** The word is rooted in the 16th-century Scottish borders, where “mail” — from the Old Norse māl, meaning “agreement” — was the word for rent or payment. “Black mail” was protection money paid to plundering chiefs and raiders to avoid being robbed or attacked; Merriam-Webster dates the word to 1552. Back then the threat was violence, not exposure — the word came to mean extortion by threatened exposure only later, which is the sense this technique uses today.

## 4.2 Urgency & Scarcity

Artificial time or resource limits designed to trigger rushed, unexamined decisions.

### 4.2.1 Time Pressure {#time-pressure}

**Definition:** Forcing a rapid choice by emphasising limited time to decide.

**Variants:** Rushed: the deadline may be real, but it leaves no time to check. False Deadline: an artificial cutoff that doesn’t reflect any real constraint.

**Example (Rushed):** “We need your signature in the next hour.”

**Example (False Deadline):** A “sale ends at midnight” timer resets, and the same deal is back tomorrow.

**Protective Response:** Don’t decide inside the window. Say “I’ll get back to you by Thursday. Please send the terms in writing.” Real offers survive a pause.

**Caution:** A caller claiming to be your bank, the police or the tax office and demanding immediate payment is a scam. Hang up, then contact the organisation yourself using an official number. See “Where to get help”: Scams and fraud, Consumer rights.

### 4.2.2 FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) {#fomo}

**Definition:** Social-pressure variant of scarcity that leverages status, trendiness, or peer behaviour to push decisions. The lever is what you’ll miss, not the clock.

**Example:** “Everyone’s already joining — don’t be the only one left out.”

**Protective Response:** Ask what you’d actually lose by waiting, and check the “everyone” claim. You can say “I’ll pass for now. If it’s still open later, I’ll look again.”

**Did you know?** Marketing strategist Dan Herman used the phrase ‘fear of missing out’ in a 2000 paper. Patrick McGinnis used the acronym FOMO in a 2004 opinion piece as a Harvard Business School student. Accounts differ on who coined it.

## 4.3 Deceptive Framing

Shaping the context or choices so every available option benefits the manipulator.

### 4.3.1 Bait-and-Switch {#bait-and-switch}

**Definition:** Lure with one promise, deliver something else after you commit.

**Example:** A low-priced service advertised, then you’re told “that was just the setup fee — ongoing costs apply.”

**Protective Response:** Get full terms in writing and screenshot the original offer. Don’t pay more to “fix” it. You can say “That’s not the offer I agreed to. I’ll take the original terms or cancel.”

**Did you know?** The practice is almost certainly older than its name — in Britain it’s also known as “switch selling”. It’s now formally banned as a deceptive trade practice in consumer-protection law in many countries — not just frowned upon, but explicitly illegal.

### 4.3.2 Anchoring {#anchoring}

**Definition:** Presenting an extreme reference point to make subsequent offers seem reasonable by comparison. It works on your sense of value, so no one needs to refuse anything. Distinct from Door-in-the-Face, which depends on you saying no first.

**Example:** A menu that lists a £120 bottle first makes the £45 bottle feel moderate.

**Protective Response:** Decide your own number before you hear theirs. You can then say “I’ve seen this at about £X elsewhere. Could you match that?”

**Did you know?** Documented by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in their landmark 1974 paper on judgement under uncertainty. Kahneman later won a Nobel Prize for related work.

### 4.3.3 Loaded Questions {#loaded-questions}

**Definition:** Asking questions that assume a premise so answering implicitly accepts that premise.

**Example:** “Why are you always so slow to respond?” assumes you’re slow.

**Protective Response:** Don’t answer as asked. Answer the real fact, or pause.

**Caution:** In a police interview, disciplinary or legal setting, ask for advice or a representative before answering. See “Where to get help”: Formal interviews, Work.

**Did you know?** In a 1974 experiment, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people films of car crashes. Those asked how fast the cars were going when they “smashed into” each other gave higher estimates than those asked about “hit” (an average of 40.5 mph against 34 mph). In a second experiment, a week later, 32% of the “smashed” group said they had seen broken glass that was never there, against 14% of the “hit” group.

### 4.3.4 False Dilemma {#false-dilemma}

**Definition:** Presenting only two options when more exist, forcing the target to choose within a frame that benefits the manipulator regardless of which option is selected. Distinct from Loaded Questions, which hide an assumption rather than options.

**Example:** “You’re either with us or against us.” / “You can support this proposal or you can admit you don’t care about the team.”

**Protective Response:** Don’t pick from the two. “I need to think about it.” Work out the third option privately.

**Caution:** Where disagreeing counts as disloyalty, that’s a warning sign about the group.

# 5 Compliance Engineering

These tactics nudge people into desired behaviour patterns using behavioural psychology. Once compliance becomes habit, no argument is needed.

## 5.1 Sequential Compliance

Manipulation through ordered requests — each step softens resistance for the next.

### 5.1.1 Foot-in-the-Door {#foot-in-the-door}

**Definition:** Start with a small, easy request that gains commitment, then escalate to a bigger one.

**Example:** “Can you sign this petition?” → later, “Can you donate to the cause?”

**Protective Response:** Treat each ask as a new decision. Having said yes before creates no obligation. Pause before answering.

**Caution:** In a close relationship, watch the direction over time. Small concessions about your phone, money or who you see that keep growing are a control pattern, not a series of favours. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control.

**Did you know?** Demonstrated in a real 1966 study — Freedman and Fraser got homeowners to agree to a small safe-driving sign in their window first, which made them far more likely to accept a large, intrusive garden sign later.

### 5.1.2 Door-in-the-Face {#door-in-the-face}

**Definition:** Begin with an unreasonable request, get rejected, then follow with a smaller one that feels like a compromise. It works through obligation: having apparently given something up, they expect you to meet them halfway. Distinct from Anchoring, which needs no refusal.

**Example:** “Can you volunteer every weekend?” → “Okay, just one day then.”

**Protective Response:** Ask yourself whether you’d agree to the smaller ask if it had been the first thing asked. Decide by your own limits, not by comparison. Opening high is also ordinary negotiation, and you can’t tell intent from outside. The response is the same either way.

**Did you know?** First demonstrated by Robert Cialdini in 1975 — people who’d refused a two-year volunteer commitment were far more likely to agree to a single trip to the zoo.

### 5.1.3 Sunk-Cost Exploitation {#sunk-cost-exploitation}

**Definition:** Playing on what you’ve already invested — time, money, effort, or feelings — so that walking away feels like throwing it all away. Distinct from Foot-in-the-Door, which escalates the next request — this leans on what you’ve already spent, so leaving becomes the thing that feels costly.

**Example:** “You’ve already put £2,000 into this — stop now and it was all for nothing.”

**Protective Response:** Ask “Knowing what I know now, would I start this today?” Judge only what happens from here, and get an outside view before spending more. Anyone who keeps reminding you of your investment is worth a second look.

**Caution:** “You’ve already paid this much, one more fee releases your money” is a standard investment and romance scam. Stop paying and keep the messages. See “Where to get help”: Scams and fraud.

**Did you know?** The related “Concorde fallacy” is named after the supersonic airliner: the British and French governments kept funding it long after it was clear it would never pay its way, partly because they had already spent so much.

## 5.2 Social Conditioning

Shaping behaviour by exploiting social instincts: belonging, status, and respect for authority.

### 5.2.1 Social Proof {#social-proof}

**Definition:** Making you think “everyone else is doing it” so you conform automatically.

**Example:** “Nine out of ten people choose this plan.”

**Protective Response:** Ask privately where the figure comes from, who counted and how. Check independent reviews. Popular and true aren’t the same thing.

**Caution:** Ratings and reviews can be faked, so treat a pile of five-star reviews as unverified. See “Where to get help”: Consumer rights.

**Did you know?** The term itself was popularised by Robert Cialdini in his 1984 book Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion.

### 5.2.2 Authority Bias {#authority-bias}

**Definition:** Leveraging titles, uniforms, or credentials to override your judgement.

**Example:** “The expert says this is perfectly safe.”

**Protective Response:** Check that the credential relates to this claim and whether independent experts agree. Respect real expertise, then verify. Take time before agreeing.

**Caution:** Don’t stop medication or treatment because someone challenged it; check with your doctor or pharmacist. If someone claims to be the police, your bank or the tax office and demands payment, hang up and contact them yourself using an official number. At work, challenge a manager through the formal route. See “Where to get help”: Medical or authority claims, Scams and fraud.

**Did you know?** Directly tied to Stanley Milgram’s 1961–63 obedience experiments, in which ordinary people delivered what they believed were dangerous electric shocks simply because someone in a lab coat told them to.

### 5.2.3 Conformity Pressure {#conformity-pressure}

**Definition:** Using group norms or peer behaviour to suppress dissent. Distinct from Social Proof, which uses others’ behaviour as evidence of what’s correct — this uses group pressure to suppress dissent even when you know better.

**Example:** “Everyone agreed already — don’t be difficult.”

**Protective Response:** Ask “Would I agree if I were alone?” and get one outside opinion before yielding. If dissent isn’t safe, say less rather than more.

**Caution:** If dissent is met with exclusion, threats or loss of income or housing, whether in a workplace, family or community, keep contacts outside the group. See “Where to get help”: High-control groups, Work.

**Did you know?** Solomon Asch’s 1951 experiment found that people went along with a group’s obviously wrong answer about a third of the time, and three in four did so at least once, just to match a group of strangers.

### 5.2.4 False Kinship {#false-kinship}

**Definition:** Claiming or staging a shared identity — same background, faith, profession, or values — to win trust fast and make questioning them feel like betraying the group. Distinct from Social Proof, which points to what others do, and Conformity Pressure, which punishes dissent — this one uses belonging itself as the reason to trust.

***Example:** “We’re the same, you and me — people like us have to look out for each other. So trust me on this.**”***

**Protective Response:** Be friendly, but check them as you would a stranger. Notice when “people like us” arrives just before a request for money, secrecy or loyalty.

**Caution:** Affinity fraud and high-control groups work this way. If you’re asked for secrecy, money or to cut ties with outsiders, treat it as a warning sign. See “Where to get help”: High-control groups, Scams and fraud.

**Did you know?** In experiments published in 1971 by psychologist Henri Tajfel and colleagues in Bristol, teenage boys were told they’d been sorted into groups by their taste in paintings — the sorting was actually random. They still favoured their own group when handing out points, showing how little it takes to create a sense of “us”, and how quickly it starts to shape who we favour.

## 5.3 Reward/Punishment Cycles

Conditioning through fluctuating feedback that shapes future behaviour.

### 5.3.1 Intermittent Reinforcement {#intermittent-reinforcement}

**Definition:** Rewarding you on an unpredictable schedule — praise or success sometimes follows the same effort, sometimes it doesn’t, with no explanation either way. Nothing negative happens when the reward doesn’t come; it’s the not knowing that keeps you trying. The same mechanism that makes slot machines and infinite scroll hard to put down.

**Example:** A manipulative boss praises your work sometimes and says nothing other times — never negative, just unpredictable — so you find yourself overworking, chasing the next compliment.

**Protective Response:** Track outcomes, not reactions. Did the effort produce a result, or just a compliment? Keep your self-worth separate from variable praise. At work, you can ask “Could we agree regular feedback, so I know where I stand?”

**Caution:** The same mechanism drives slot machines, loot boxes and infinite scroll. If gambling feels out of control, get support. See “Where to get help”: Gambling.

**Did you know?** This is B.F. Skinner’s 1950s operant conditioning research — the same mechanism casino slot machines are deliberately engineered around.

### 5.3.2 Variable Approval {#variable-approval}

**Definition:** Alternating unpredictably between acceptance and rejection to keep you unstable and compliant. Distinct from Conditional Regard, where affection tracks a known condition — comply and you’re warm, don’t and you’re cold — here the swings don’t reliably track anything you did. Also distinct from Intermittent Reinforcement, which only ever withholds reward and never tips into actual rejection: Variable Approval swings all the way into disapproval, not just silence.

**Example:** “You’re amazing!” one day, cold silence the next — for the exact same behaviour, with no way to tell which one you’ll get.

**Protective Response:** Keep a private log of dates, what you did and the response, to see if the swings track anything. Build support outside the relationship, and don’t tune your behaviour to predict their mood.

**Caution:** Unpredictable warmth and coldness, especially alongside fear, is a recognised pattern in abusive relationships. See “Where to get help”: Abuse or coercive control.
